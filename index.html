Pick Me!

 

 

Chapter One: Ada

 

 

On Why Men Should Not Be Allowed in the Kitchen

 

 

I want to start by saying that I love men. I feel like I have to say that now, because the minute a woman says anything with a backbone, somebody decides she's bitter. I'm not bitter. I'm the least bitter person I know. Ask anyone. Ask my husband.

 

 

His name is Daniel. We met at a friend's engagement party fourteen years ago, when I was twenty-three and he was the only man in the room who refilled my drink without being asked. I remember thinking, this one's been raised right. We dated for three years and we've been married for eleven, and in all that time I can count on one hand the number of real fights we've had, and I'll tell you right now that none of them were about the kitchen. That's not luck. That's architecture.

 

 

Because I have a rule in my house, and I've had it since the day we moved in together, and I'm going to tell you about it even though I know how it sounds. Men are not allowed in my kitchen. Not as guests, not as helpers, not as "let me just grab a glass of water." I'll get you the water. Sit down.

 

 

People hear that and they assume it's about him. It isn't. It's about me.

 

 

Here is something nobody tells you about the kitchen. It's the only room in the house where a woman is still in charge.

 

 

Think about it honestly. The living room doesn't belong to anybody. It belongs to the television, to whoever's visiting, to whichever child is loudest, to the dog if you have a dog. It's a public square. You can arrange the cushions however you like in the morning and by evening it's been rearranged by six other people and a remote control. You don't own the living room. You just tidy it.

 

 

The bedroom, now. I know there are women who'll argue the bedroom is theirs. They'll point to the throw pillows, the candle on the nightstand, the paint color they picked out from a fan of little cards. And I understand that. But I'd ask you to look a little harder. Who decides what time the lights go off? Whose alarm goes off first, and whose alarm gets silenced so it doesn't wake him? Who sleeps on the side nearest the door, and who gets up when there's a noise? Who sets the pace in there, when we're being frank about what bedrooms are for? Picking the pillows isn't ownership. A tenant picks the curtains. The bedroom runs on his clock, his sleep, his body, his comfort, and we decorate it so we don't have to notice. I'm not complaining. I like our bedroom. I'm just not going to pretend it's mine.

 

 

The garage is his. The car is his. The thermostat, my God, the thermostat. Even the bathroom is a negotiation, and the negotiation is mostly about how long he can be in there with the door shut and a magazine.

 

 

But the kitchen? Nobody wants the kitchen. It's the one territory they never bothered to conquer, and I'll tell you what, I'm not handing it over now just because some magazine decided that a man holding a whisk is "sexy."

 

 

I've seen those photos. The man in the apron, sleeves rolled up, laughing at something off camera with flour on his nose. You know what I see when I look at that? I see a woman just out of frame who's going to be cleaning flour off the ceiling fan at eleven o'clock at night. That man is not cooking. He is performing cooking. There's a difference, and the difference is who wipes down the counters.

 

 

My sister-in-law, Becca, lets her husband cook. She says it's "partnership." I've eaten at Becca's house. He makes one thing. It's chili. He has been making this chili for nine years and it has never once gotten better, and every time he makes it the entire family has to act like he's come down from a mountain with tablets. "Oh, Mike's making his chili!" Meanwhile Becca does the rice, the cornbread, the salad, the dessert, the shopping, the dishes, and then she thanks him. I watched her thank him. I'm not judging her. I actually think Becca is very brave, in her way. She has decided that the way to be happy is to be grateful for the chili. That's a choice. I've just made a different one.

 

 

Because here's what happens when you let a man into the kitchen. He doesn't become your equal in there. He becomes your project. Where's the garlic press. Is this the good pan. How long does chicken go in for. Did you want me to do the onions in slices or, like, chunks. Suddenly you're not cooking, you're teaching, and you're teaching someone who doesn't want to learn, he wants to be seen learning, which is a completely different thing and takes twice as long.

 

 

And then, and this is the part nobody talks about, he's worse at it than you, and you have to pretend he isn't. You have to eat the undersalted thing. You have to say "no, this is great." You have to manage his feelings about the food while eating the food. That's not a partnership. That's a second job with a worse boss.

 

 

I'd rather just do it myself. That's not oppression. That's efficiency.

 

 

I know what some of you are thinking. "Ada, isn't it a little old-fashioned?" And I want to push back on that word, old-fashioned, because I think it's gotten lazy. My grandmother was old-fashioned. She cooked for eleven people and had no say over the money. I have total say over the money. Daniel doesn't even know where I keep the checkbook. I did that on purpose. You think a woman who manages a household budget down to the penny and runs a kitchen like a ship's galley is old-fashioned? No. I'm the opposite. I've just figured out which rooms are worth fighting over.

 

 

And frankly, women who insist their husbands "help" in the kitchen, I think a lot of them are insecure. I'll say it. I think they need him in there because they need a witness. They need someone to see how hard it is. I don't need a witness. I know how hard it is. I do it at 6 a.m. and I do it at 7 p.m. and I do it on Christmas Eve with my hair in a clip and nobody sees any of it and that's fine. That's what competence looks like. It's invisible. If it weren't invisible, it would mean I was doing it wrong.

 

 

Daniel tried once, early on, the first year we lived together. Pancakes. He woke me up to show me. I want you to sit with that for a second. He made a Sunday breakfast and the first thing he did was wake up the person it was supposedly for, so that she could watch him flip it. I smiled. I said they were wonderful. I ate two. Then I got up and did the dishes because he'd used, I counted, four bowls. Four bowls for pancakes. And he came up behind me while I was scrubbing and kissed my neck and said "see, I can help," and I thought, no, sweetheart, you can't, and that's okay, that's actually okay, I'd rather you couldn't.

 

 

Because if he could, then what would I be?

 

 

That came out wrong. What I mean is, a house needs a center. Someone has to be the one who knows where the tape measure is and when the milk goes bad and which cousin can't have shellfish. That's me. That's power. Real power, not the kind you get from a corner office. You can fire a CEO. You can't fire the only person who knows how the oven timer works.

 

 

So no. No men in the kitchen. Not because they'd do it badly, although they would. Not because it's "my place," which is a phrase I've never once used. Because I have built something in there, over eleven years, that is exactly the way I want it, and I have asked for nothing else in this house, not the thermostat, not the remote, not the weekends, not the bedroom, and I'm not about to let somebody wander in with flour on his nose and call it generosity.

 

 

Let him sit down. I'll bring him a plate. He'll say it's delicious.

 

 

And he'll be right. It always is.
